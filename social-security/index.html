How this works and what it leaves out
  • Lifetime value. Each year's Social Security and IRA withdrawals minus the federal (and state) income tax they cause, plus whatever IRA is left at the last death after your heirs' tax, all discounted at your return on savings and weighted by the chance you are alive. Taking benefits early and spending them leaves your savings invested, so the same return applies whether you spend or invest the checks. Pensions and other income count toward tax brackets but are the same in every plan, so they are left out of the value.
  • IRA bridge. With the bridge on, Social Security plus IRA withdrawals are held at a level amount each year (your full-age benefits plus planned withdrawals). Delaying Social Security means drawing more from the IRA in your 60s, which shrinks required minimum distributions later (starting at 73, or 75 if born in 1960 or later). Any shortfall from a benefit cut is also filled from the IRA. With the bridge off, you take the planned amount or the required minimum, whichever is larger. The IRA grows at your return on savings. State tax on IRA withdrawals is not modeled.
  • Roth vs. Traditional. Each year's withdrawal is split between the accounts by the percentage you set. Roth withdrawals are tax-free and don't count toward the income that makes Social Security taxable. Required minimums apply only to the Traditional account (Roth 401(k)s no longer have them), so the Traditional withdrawal is never below the minimum. If one account runs out, the other covers the rest. Roth money left to heirs is valued tax-free; Traditional money is reduced by the heirs' tax rate.
  • Trust fund cut. When set, every benefit paid from the chosen year on, including spousal and survivor benefits, is reduced by that percentage. It is a what-if; Congress has always acted before past shortfalls.
  • Tax. Up to 85% of benefits become taxable once "provisional income" (other income + tax-exempt interest + half your benefits) passes $25,000 single or $32,000 married. Those thresholds are not indexed, so more of your benefit is taxed each year. Uses 2026 brackets and standard deduction indexed for inflation, the extra deduction for 65+, and the temporary $6,000 senior deduction through 2028. Other income is treated as ordinary income.
  • Earnings test. If you claim before full retirement age and keep working, $1 is withheld for every $2 earned over $24,480 (2026). Withheld months are credited back as a higher benefit at full retirement age.
  • State tax. Eight states still tax some benefits in 2026 (CO, CT, MN, MT, NM, RI, UT, VT); each one's exemption rules are modeled in simplified form, with the rate applied to the federally taxable portion. The pre-filled rate is a typical middle-income rate; adjust it to your bracket. Every other state, including West Virginia from 2026, exempts benefits. Thresholds and rates as published for tax year 2026.
  • Married couples. Includes spousal top-ups (up to 50% of the other's full benefit, starting once both have filed) and survivor benefits (the widow(er) keeps the larger of the two checks). The survivor files as single.
  • Lifespan. "Realistic range" spreads each person's age at death around the guess using a standard mortality curve, so a guess of 87 includes real chances of dying at 75 or living to 97. "Exactly" assumes the guess comes true.
  • Simplified. Whole-year ages with January birthdays; no IRMAA Medicare surcharges, ACA premium subsidies, capital-gains rates, Roth conversions, or taxable brokerage accounts. Your benefit estimate should come from your statement at ssa.gov. This is an educational estimate, not financial or tax advice.
